Getting Started with AI

The take-home guide from the 2026 faculty retreat AI session. Ten minutes to read; the habits take a week.

The one rule before anything else. Patient information (PHI), student records, and other regulated data go only into Michigan Medicine–approved tools such as U-M GPT and Maizey. Consumer chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot personal accounts) are for everything else. When in doubt, don't paste it — ask. Current institutional guidance lives at genai.umich.edu.

2. How to ask — the whole method

Prompt engineering is over. Current models are trained past the 2023 tricks ("you are a world-class expert," "take a deep breath," "I'll tip you $200"). What works is what works with a smart new colleague:

  1. 1Say what you want. The outcome, not the steps. "Make me a one-page summary for the chair" beats "Please write a document that…"
  2. 2Give the facts. Paste the raw material — dates, names, the messy draft, the constraint that matters. It cannot read your mind, but it reads everything you give it.
  3. 3Ask for a draft to review. "Make a draft version for me to review" keeps you in charge and lowers the stakes of the first attempt.
  4. 4Invite questions back. End with the single most useful sentence in this guide:
Ask me any questions you have before you get started.

It surfaces the ambiguity you didn't know was there, before an hour of work goes the wrong direction.

Talk instead of type

Voice input on the phone apps is the lowest-friction way to prompt. Ramble; it sorts it out. Dictating a messy three-minute description usually beats a carefully typed sentence.

For big projects: let Chat write the prompt

Don't hand-write a long brief. Describe the project to Claude or ChatGPT in a few sentences, tell it to ask you questions first, then ask it to write the kickoff prompt for the agent (Claude Code, Cowork). Four sentences in, five pages out — and it will remember details you'd have forgotten. If you've already been working in a chat for weeks, ask that chat for a handoff document with all key decisions and give the file to the new session.

3. Trust, but verify — in 60 seconds

Accuracy was our #1 concern in the survey (83% of us). The right response isn't avoidance; it's a minute of checking.

  1. Ask where it came from. "Show me the source for that number" — then open one yourself.
  2. Spot-check some figures against the raw file. A few checks calibrate the whole answer.
  3. Never cite an unopened reference. Fabricated citations look perfect.
  4. Get a second opinion: paste the draft into a fresh chat — "find what's wrong here."
  5. The signature test: if you wouldn't sign it, don't send it.

Corollary: a model that asked you good clarifying questions is not therefore correct.

4. Disclosure — where we stand

83% of us want AI use disclosed in manuscripts; 62% in figures and images; over half in clinical notes. Until the department publishes a formal norm, the working rule:

  • Manuscripts, abstracts, grants: follow the journal's or sponsor's policy (most now follow ICMJE: disclose use in methods or acknowledgments; AI is never an author; you are responsible for every word and citation).
  • Figures and images: disclose any AI generation or manipulation; never for data figures without stating exactly what was done.
  • Clinical documentation: follow the institutional ambient-AI consent and attestation workflow; you sign the note, you own the note.
  • Slides, letters, emails: no disclosure expected for editing and formatting help — use judgment when the content itself is AI-generated.

5. Getting unstuck

  • Start with the prompt library — copy a prompt, swap in your details, go.
  • Ask the AI itself. "I'm a urologist who has never used you before. What are three things you could take off my plate this week? Ask me questions first."
  • Ask a colleague who presented at the retreat — Michael Uy, Kristian Stensland, Todd Morgan, Kristen Chrouser, Rod Dunn. Every one of us was a beginner recently.
  • Give it thirty unhurried minutes once. The most common free-text request in our survey was "time and space to play around with it." Block it on a calendar; treat it as CME.
